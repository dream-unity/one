01 · Breathe

Heart and feeling

Breathe gently. Feel love.
Keep the feeling and heart area together.

Rest attention in the middle of your chest. Breathe gently: five seconds in, five seconds out. Invite a real feeling of love. Keep that feeling and the heart area in mind at the same time.

  1. Find the heart area. Let the chest soften. Imagine breathing through this place. The air still enters your lungs.
  2. Let the feeling come. Think of someone, an animal, or a place you love. Notice what the memory brings into your body. The aim is to feel love, not only think the word.
  3. Keep both together. Hold the feeling and heart attention as you breathe. Notice finer parts. Let it grow deeper, steadier, or stronger without strain. If it fades, invite it again.
If the feeling is hard to find

Choose a small, real moment of care. Let the picture help the feeling begin. Then attend to what you actually feel. You can feel love without a warm chest, or warmth without love. Do not invent a sensation to match a prompt. Ease can come first; keep practising honestly.

Which feeling will you practise?

Start with love. Try each of the other feelings in later sessions. Keep attention in the heart area with every one. Compare their full patterns; they need not feel the same.

How much time?
Choose a shorter practice

The same three stages fit your chosen time. Long sessions are available, not a higher score or a proven better dose.

Choose an easy pace

If following the circle feels hard, choose your own breath. Slower is not a harder level.

Do not take huge breaths or hold your breath. If you feel dizzy, short of breath or uncomfortable, stop and breathe as usual.

If attention to the heart feels distressing, open your eyes, shorten the session, and let ease lead before love.

Choose a sound

Sound is optional. Choose what you like and keep it quiet. You can turn all sound off during practice.

Choose the pulse speed. Hz means pulses each second. Low rates (1–3) use a darker tone; higher rates (8–12) use a brighter tone.

What changes between sounds?

The first three use slower pulses, settling at 1, 2 or 3 cycles a second. The next three settle at 8, 10 or 12. The sound slowly changes as it plays. A cycle per second is also called one hertz, or Hz.

These are sound settings, not your brain’s speed. The mix uses low tones, binaural beats (a different tone in each ear), and pulses of loudness. Headphones separate the ears for the binaural part; the pulse and bell can also play through speakers. The mix has not been shown to put people into a set brain state. The volume number cannot tell us how loud your headphones are. Read about quiet listening (opens a new tab).

Compare two waysA personal comparison · 4 minutes, plus a few taps

Try breathing with heart attention alone and breathing with your chosen felt emotion and heart attention. Make a guess first. Then try each way twice, for one minute each.

Keep the same easy pace and sound. The page picks which way comes first. After each round, tap how easy it felt. “Not clear” and “Skip” both count as honest answers.

You will see your four reports. They cannot prove what caused a change. What you expect, what came before, and changes in breathing may still matter.

Why repeat and change the order?

One way comes first and last. The other comes in the middle. Next time, the page may swap them. Each gets the same practice time.

This helps you question a simple “the last thing worked best” story. It does not remove every effect of order. A felt emotion can carry into the next round. You also know which way you are trying.

Decide what you are comparing before you start: how easy the practice feels. That is a report of experience, not a heart, hormone or brain measurement.

The science and the questions

How heart and feeling work together.

Keep the full practice: an easy breath, attention in the heart area, and a felt emotion together. Learn to call up love, keep it present, and explore it more closely. These questions explain the science, the open questions, and ways to test your own learning.

What am I learning to do?

Try holding two things in attention at once. Notice the area near your heart. At the same time, bring forward the feeling of love. Keep both present as you breathe. This is more than thinking the word “love”. You are learning to recognise and sustain the experience that the word points to.

A memory can help you begin. Think of someone, an animal, or a place you care about. Notice what happens in your body. Once a feeling comes, see whether you can stay with it without replaying the whole story. If it fades, use the memory again. A small feeling is a starting point, not a failed attempt.

In this practice, activation means deliberately bringing a felt state into attention. It does not mean switching on a heart muscle or measuring a particular nerve. Calling it up, keeping it present, returning after a distraction, and carrying it into daily life are separate skills. Our exact sequence remains an experimental training design.

How are love, thanks, appreciation and compassion different?

Love: “You matter to me.” Begin with someone or something you care about. Gratitude: “Something good was given to me.” Recall a real act of help. Appreciation: “I notice something good.” Stay with a small thing you value. Compassion: “You are having a hard time, and I want to help.” Begin with a manageable difficulty.

Try each feeling while keeping attention in the same heart area. Give each the same time. Ask what changes: warmth, softness, energy, steadiness, or a wish to act? You might notice another quality, a mixture, or no clear change. The examples are invitations, not answers you must produce.

These are related feelings with different aims. Research distinguishes caring about suffering from sharing another person's distress. Compassion training changed feeling reports and brain responses differently from empathy training. This supports exploring care carefully; it does not give every emotion one fixed bodily signature. Read the compassion and empathy experiment.

What does “breathe through the heart” mean?

Air goes into your lungs. “Through the heart” is an image that helps you keep attention near the centre of your chest while breathing. You can use the image and still know how breathing really works.

A chest feeling can include movement from breathing, skin warmth, muscle tension, and sensations related to a heartbeat. Feeling something there does not tell us which tissue made it. You do not need to detect each beat to practise holding chest attention and love together.

People draw some shared patterns when asked where they feel emotions in the body. But those drawings are reports of experience, not scans of organs. Your love need not feel like someone else's love. Describe what is there before deciding what it means. Read the study of body maps of emotion.

Why five seconds in and five seconds out?

Five seconds in and five out makes one breath every ten seconds: six breaths a minute, or 0.1 hertz. Hertz means cycles each second. This is the breathing rhythm, not a heart beating once every ten seconds.

Breathing changes pressure inside the chest and helps change the gaps between heartbeats. Heart rate often rises as you breathe in and falls as you breathe out. This breathing-linked change is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia. Here, that name describes a normal rhythm.

Your body also checks blood pressure. Stretch sensors in large arteries send messages that help adjust heart rate and blood vessels. This feedback loop is the baroreflex. Its responses take time. Near certain breathing speeds, the breath and this loop reinforce a larger repeating wave, like well-timed pushes on a swing. Researchers call that resonance. The rhythms need not all reach their peaks together. Read how the breathing and pressure loops interact.

Is there one best breathing speed?

Six breaths a minute is a useful starting rhythm in adult research. It is not a rule that every body must obey. In measured biofeedback, a trained practitioner can compare several comfortable speeds while recording breathing and the heart. A screen animation alone cannot find your personal resonance.

A 2026 trial assigned 88 adults to personal resonance breathing, fixed six-breaths-a-minute training, or a control group. Both training groups reported fewer stress, anxiety and depression symptoms. The study found no clear advantage for personal calibration and no improvement in resting HRV. Its size did not settle whether a small difference exists. Read the four-week comparison.

Nor is a longer out-breath automatically better. Two small experiments at six breaths a minute found no clear HRV advantage for twice-as-long out-breaths over equal in-and-out breaths. Keep the original five-and-five rhythm comfortable. Slower, deeper and longer are not scores for skill. Read the breathing-ratio experiments.

What does a smooth heart wave tell us?

Heart rate variability, or HRV, means variation in the time between beats. Heart rate tells us how fast the heart beats. They answer different questions. A “coherent” heart rhythm usually means that much of this variation follows one repeating wave.

A smooth wave can arise from paced breathing. It cannot, by itself, identify love, prove honesty, measure wisdom, or show that every brain and body system is working in harmony. Different coherence scores also use different calculations.

Researchers divide heart-rhythm waves into frequency bands. At six breaths a minute, the breathing wave moves into the low-frequency band. A fall in high-frequency power then need not mean that calming nerve activity fell. The low-to-high frequency ratio is not a simple stress-versus-calm balance. Breathing, posture, recording length, missed beats and movement all matter. Read the measurement guidance.

This page has no heart sensor. Its moving shapes and timed indicators guide the exercise. They do not detect your heart, attention or feelings.

What does the HeartMath research actually show?

HeartMath combines attention near the heart, easier breathing, and a feeling such as appreciation or care. An early experiment reported different heart-rhythm patterns during appreciation and anger. That is a reason to study emotion and physiology together. It is not proof that every claimed benefit comes from emotion rather than breathing. Read the 1995 emotion experiment.

A 2025 study analysed about 1.8 million sessions from about 68,000 app users. More positive feeling reports were associated with higher coherence scores. These were repeated sessions, not 1.8 million separate people. Users were not randomly assigned to emotion practice or breath-only practice. Their breathing was not independently measured. The study therefore cannot separate changes in breathing, expectation, emotion, practice history and the effects of seeing feedback. Several authors were affiliated with HeartMath. Read the app study and its methods.

The link is interesting. Its cause remains a question. Dream Unity keeps the full felt-emotion task while making that distinction clear.

Does the heart have its own nerves and memory?

The heart has a local network of nerve cells. Anatomical research has mapped these cells and their connections. This is called the intrinsic cardiac nervous system: nerves located within the heart. They help process signals and regulate cardiac work. Counts vary with the method and tissue studied; “40,000” is not an exact count for every person. Read the human heart anatomy study.

The heart also contains specialised muscle cells that make electrical rhythms. These pacemaker cells can generate beats without a new command from the brain for every beat. A transplanted heart's ability to beat does not show that it remembers a person's love. Read the review of local cardiac control.

A nerve circuit changing with experience is one meaning of learning. Remembering a loved person is a much larger claim. The cited anatomy does not establish that emotional memories live in heart tissue. You can take a bodily feeling seriously without making that claim.

What about oxytocin and the “love hormone”?

Oxytocin is a chemical messenger. A 1998 experiment found that rat hearts made and released it. The often-quoted “nineteen times” compared oxytocin concentration in rat right-atrium tissue with rat uterine tissue. It did not measure love in a human heart, and it did not test this exercise. Read the original rat-heart experiment.

A chemical can have several jobs. Its presence in an organ does not tell us what someone feels. Nor does a warm chest tell us that oxytocin increased, cortisol fell, or a particular nerve switched on.

Research on maternal and romantic love found activity in brain systems involved in reward and attachment. Those scans help build biological explanations of love. They do not show that this breathing exercise reliably opens one hormone pathway or turns off the amygdala, a brain region involved in learning what matters. Read the brain-imaging study of love.

Can heart practice change the brain or lasting regulation?

That is plausible, and some measured training studies support parts of it. A study of 54 adults found that repeated HRV biofeedback improved the sensitivity of the blood-pressure feedback loop. It used physiological recordings; it did not test our sensor-free session. Read the baroreflex training study.

A five-week trial with 106 young adults compared training that increased heart-rate waves with an active comparison. The planned main brain result, a change in right amygdala–prefrontal connection, was not significant. Some other results, including the left-side connection, changed. That is promising, mixed evidence, not proof that all brain networks become more coherent. Read the brain trial.

A months-long mental-training study also found improvement in voluntary HRV regulation, including changes after accounting for breathing, without better resting HRV. A skill during practice and a lasting resting change are different outcomes. Read the mental-training study.

Can practising care change cells or ageing?

Telomeres are protective ends on chromosomes, the packages holding genetic instructions. A 2019 trial assigned 142 adults to loving-kindness training, mindfulness training, or waiting. It measured telomere length before and after the programme. Loving-kindness showed less shortening than waiting. Mindfulness fell between the other groups and did not differ significantly from either. Read the trial, correction and linked scientific comment.

Here is the subtle point: finding a clear change in one group and no clear change in another does not, by itself, prove the groups changed differently. The direct comparison matters. This study does not establish that love has a unique cellular benefit that attention lacks.

A short-term change in one blood-cell measure also cannot establish longer life or slower ageing of the whole body. This research keeps the biological question open. It does not turn our exact exercise into an anti-ageing treatment.

Why practise a real feeling instead of performing one?

Because the target is your experience. Saying “I feel love” while feeling nothing trains a report, not the skill you came to practise. A memory or kind wish can help a feeling develop. That is a valid way to begin; deliberate does not mean false.

There is no simple body test for sincerity. Even smiles with crinkled eyes can be made deliberately. A facial movement cannot settle whether a feeling is true. Read the experiment on smiles and positive emotion.

In a study assigning 135 people to different short exercises, self-compassion practices produced patterns of bodily calming alongside changes in reported care. It studied supported exercises, not a universal test for love. Read the self-compassion experiment.

Try honestly. If love is hard to reach, begin with appreciation or a little care. Notice the difference between wanting a feeling, imagining it, and actually feeling something.

What can “deeper” mean as I improve?

Keep love and the heart area in attention together. Then explore one change at a time: clearer recognition; steadier attention; less effort; a finer distinction between emotions; returning after distraction; bringing the feeling forward with less help from a story; or carrying care into an ordinary action.

These are proposed training targets, not proven stages everyone passes through. A feeling can become stronger, but strength is only one feature. A quiet feeling held clearly can be more skilful than a powerful feeling that takes over attention.

Try letting the picture fade while keeping the feeling. Next time, practise with a different safe memory. Later, see whether you can keep some care while looking around. Compare clarity, effort and choice separately. Do not assume that a longer session makes the feeling stronger, or that stronger always helps.

Ask yourself: Can I bring this feeling forward, stay with it, and also let it go by choice?

How can I test what the feeling adds?

The full exercise stays intact. A separate comparison can explore its parts. Before starting, choose one question: Does felt love make the practice easier? Does it make returning to the heart area easier? These are different questions.

Keep duration, sound, posture and breathing pace alike. Compare heart-area attention with and without deliberately calling up love. Repeat both, and change their order across sessions. Record your guess first. Afterwards, report what happened, including “no change” or “unclear”.

A feeling may carry into the next round. You know which exercise you are doing. Breathing depth can change even at the same pace. So a personal comparison helps you learn, but cannot prove cause or a body mechanism.

A stronger research study could cross breathing pace with emotion instructions, measure actual breathing and heart signals, use equal support in every group, and decide its main outcome in advance. It should also test later recall, daily-life transfer and discomfort. Each addition asks whether the full training works, how it works, and for whom.

What do the sounds add?

A bell can mark a change without making you read. A repeating sound can help you keep time. Those are useful jobs even if it does not change a brain wave.

A sound's pulse speed, your breathing speed, and measured brain rhythms are different things. Choosing a sound labelled 10 hertz does not show your brain is running at 10 hertz. Binaural beats also require different tones delivered to the two ears; they are not the same as any pulsing sound.

Some experiments have reported physiological or attention effects. Others found no enhancement of the target brain rhythms or arousal. The result depends on the sound and task. Evidence for one recording cannot validate this page's exact changing mix. Read a controlled test of brain rhythms and arousal. Read a newer test of sound settings.

Choose quiet sound or silence. Compare them if you wish; preference and measured benefit are different questions.

How do I keep a demanding practice comfortable?

Keep the breath easy and unforced. Slow breathing can still move too much air if every breath becomes very large. That can lower carbon dioxide and cause dizziness or tingling. In a randomised experiment, a simple instruction to avoid overly deep breathing reduced this problem. Read the breathing-instruction experiment.

If you feel dizzy, short of breath, distressed, or overwhelmed, stop pacing and return to ordinary breathing. Open your eyes and look around. You can stop the session. If chest attention feels distressing, let ease lead before love. Do not treat discomfort as proof that the training is working.

Long sessions are available for choice, not as a proven better dose. Adult breathing studies do not establish the right pace or duration for a young child. A child can use the clear instructions with a trusted adult, ordinary breathing and a brief practice suited to them.

Map the feeling

Feel the whole pattern.
Keep the clues and their meaning apart.

Choose a body feeling you can stay with comfortably. If you have just practised love and heart attention, keep both present and explore that pattern here.

Follow seven questions. Spend 30 seconds on each. Answer in your mind, or tap an answer where offered. Each round brings you back through the whole pattern.

  1. Where is it?
  2. What does it feel like?
  3. How strong is the sensation?
  4. Is it moving?
  5. Which way does it go?
  6. Do the body, image, wish to act, and emotion agree?
  7. Is it pleasant, unpleasant, neither, or mixed?
How much time?

All lengths use the full seven-question loop. A round takes 3 minutes 30 seconds; the final round may end partway through. Longer sessions give you more rounds, with the same depth of observation.

Breathe as usual. Notice what is present without making a feared sensation stronger. If attention feels distressing, open your eyes, feel the floor, or finish. Children practise with a trusted grown-up and can finish after a single question.

Feel, ask, test

Learn the shape of a feeling.

Keep the whole pattern. Learn one clear part at a time.

What am I feeling inside my body?

Your body has several ways to send you news. Interoception means sensing its inner condition: a heartbeat, hunger or breathing effort. Proprioception helps you know where your limbs are and how they move. Somatosensation covers body senses such as touch, pressure and temperature. These jobs overlap; scientists draw their boundaries in different ways. Studies test several channels separately because one score need not describe them all. Six body senses tested (2018); Different sensing channels (2022).

A hand on your chest adds touch. Your ribs move with breath. A pulse may be felt through nearby tissue. All can help you find this region. None alone proves that a feeling comes only from heart muscle. Here, heart attention means attending to the chest around your heart while noticing which clues you actually have.

Ask yourselfAm I noticing pressure, movement, a pulse, an image, or a mixture?

Why keep all seven questions?

A feeling has more than a size. The seven questions keep its place, shape, timing and meaning in view. They are Dream Unity’s practice structure, not a medical test.

  1. Where? Find its place and edges. Is it deep, near the skin, spread out or hard to locate?
  2. What is it like? Notice heat, pressure, weight, tingling, tightness, emptiness or another texture.
  3. How strong? Rate the body sensation. Then notice whether the emotion feels stronger or weaker than that sensation.
  4. How does it move? Notice pulses, waves, flickers, spreading or stillness. Watch when it begins and fades.
  5. Which way? Notice any felt inward, outward, upward or downward pull. A felt direction need not be material moving that way.
  6. Do the parts agree? Compare four layers: body sensation, mental image, urge to act and emotional tone. Keep mixed patterns; do not force agreement.
  7. What is its tone? Pleasant, unpleasant, neither or mixed? Drawn toward something, pulled away or still? Try an emotion name only after this full walk.

Here, the sixth question’s coherence means how those layers fit together. It does not mean measured heart-rhythm coherence. “Nothing clear” remains a complete answer.

Does each emotion have its own body shape?

There are shared patterns, with room for difference. Across five experiments, 701 people marked where emotions felt more or less active. The group maps differed between emotions. These were people’s reports, not scans showing where an emotion lives. They cannot decode every feeling in one person. Body maps of emotions (2014).

So map before reading an example. “My chest feels tight” describes something you noticed. “I must be afraid” adds a possible meaning. Check the situation, thoughts and urge to act too. Let your own pattern differ from the example.

Keep a personal dictionary that can change: “This sometimes goes with worry for me.” That is more useful than treating every flutter as the same emotion.

How do a body signal and a guess meet?

One scientific model says the brain keeps guessing what the body needs, then checks incoming news. A guess already in place is a prior. A mismatch is a prediction error. Precision means how much weight the system gives a clue. This is a proposed account of body–brain control, not a complete, settled explanation of every feeling. The active-inference model (2016).

Experiments show why expectations deserve attention. In a 2025 study, artificial pulse-like touch changed how strongly people rated emotion in faces. Feeling a convincing rhythm did not make it their true heart rhythm. False pulse feedback and emotion (2025).

Keep three entries apart: “What I expected”; “What I noticed”; “What I think it means.” A vivid feeling is real as an experience. Its explanation still needs checking.

What is the difference between strong, clear, sure and correct?

Strong describes a sensation’s size. Clear describes how well you can make out its details. Sure describes trust in your answer. Correct needs a suitable outside check. You can have any one without all the others.

A study with 80 people separated task performance, beliefs about body awareness, and how well confidence tracked correct answers. That last skill is metacognition: knowing when your own answer deserves trust. Accuracy, beliefs and confidence (2015).

This page can invite confidence checks. It cannot measure their accuracy without a suitable body measure and task. Returning to the same description shows repeatability, not proof. “I am unsure” can be a better judgement than a confident story.

How do I hold love and heart attention together?

The practice asks you to do both: bring forward a felt emotion and keep attending to the chest around your heart. Love remains a central training aim. Gratitude, appreciation and compassion let you explore related but different ways of caring.

A memory, imagined scene or kind wish can be a starting cue. Then ask what changes in the body. Keep the cue, bodily sensation and meaning as three connected parts. Imagery can guide this experiment without becoming proof that love is stored in heart tissue.

Question to test: Can you keep the feeling present while noticing its place, texture and movement? Does close observation strengthen it, leave it unchanged or interrupt it? All three teach you something. Try holding both again, without demanding warmth or a stronger heartbeat. This exact dual-attention training is an experimental design; the body-map studies do not establish its benefits.

What makes an activation pattern more than a snapshot?

A snapshot says “warmth in my chest”. A pattern also asks: What came first? Where did it spread? What faded? What returned when I brought back the same feeling?

Question to test: Map the same feeling when you begin, while you sustain it, and after you stop trying to sustain it. Keep all seven questions. Notice whether the feeling, image, breath and urge change together or at different times. This adds timing to the body map; it does not assume a hidden energy is moving.

Compare love with gratitude, appreciation and compassion on different rounds. Keep the posture, breath pace and sound as similar as you can. Similar shapes may hide different meanings; different shapes may belong to the same emotion. This is a personal experiment, not an established emotion classifier.

Why practise looking away and coming back?

Steady attention and freely moving attention are two training aims. Stay with one region, then notice a sound or something you see. Return and describe what is there now.

More inward attention is not automatically more accurate. In one experiment, heartbeat attention increased reports of touch both when a faint vibration occurred and when it did not. A separate study found that brief body-scan practice improved aspects of touch decisions. Different forms of attention can have different effects. These touch tasks do not establish what will happen in this exercise. Attention and touch reports (2012); Body scans and touch decisions (2013).

After returning, consider two possibilities: the body changed, or the way you attended changed. Both may have changed together.

What has training actually improved in studies?

Findings depend on the task and comparison. A randomized study with 40 people found a short-term heartbeat-perception gain after the first feedback session. It found no clear lasting group advantage across three weeks. Three weeks of heartbeat training (2022).

An eight-week body-scan study did better against no training, but did not clearly outperform an audiobook group on the main accuracy change. Having a useful activity, attention and repeated testing can matter. Body scans with two comparisons (2017).

Heartbeat counting also has a problem: people can use their usual heart-rate knowledge or estimate time instead of feeling beats. We do not turn such counting into an accuracy score. What heartbeat counting can measure (2018).

What might change in the brain?

A small study followed 22 healthy young people through interoceptive training. Changes in heartbeat-task performance accompanied changes in the way several brain regions’ activity varied together. These included the insula, which helps process body information, and regions involved in attention and control. An exploratory brain-imaging study (2024).

This is a clue about a possible mechanism. These before-and-after links do not by themselves show that training alone caused the changes. A brain scan also does not show that a person has mastered every body sense. This page has not been tested with that brain-imaging method.

Ask yourselfWhat would a comparison group need to do before we could credit this particular practice?

Will one body skill help everywhere?

Transfer means learning that helps with something new. In a 2026 study of 241 people, most tested heart-sensing and breath-sensing abilities did not go together. Confidence showed more overlap. This compared people at one time; it did not prove training can never transfer. Heart and breath skills compared (2026).

A separate 2026 study tested heart sensing in 456 people and breathing in 245. Measured performance was largely unrelated to mental-health symptoms. Better sensing scores do not automatically mean better mental health. Body-task performance and mental health (2026).

Test transfer directly: can you notice an early feeling during an ordinary conversation and choose your next action? Treat a helpful moment as an observation, not proof that every part of the practice caused it.

What happens between sensation and meaning?

Follow four parts: the bodily feeling; what grabs your attention; the explanation you give it; what you want to do. This is a way to study experience, not a claim that the brain always follows four separate steps.

For example: a flutter; attention on tomorrow; “something will go wrong”; an urge to avoid. Another explanation might be excitement, tiredness or a mixture. Check what is happening around you before choosing.

A label can change the experience it describes. An imaging experiment found changed brain responses when people named emotions in negative pictures. It did not show that a word identifies the true cause of a chest sensation. Naming emotions in a picture task (2007).

Try “This may be worry.” Ask which new clue would make you change that name.

How far do treatment and child studies reach?

A trial with 121 autistic adults found a modest anxiety benefit from six sessions with heartbeat feedback and controlled physical activity. Only half supplied the three-month follow-up; one person reported cardiac anxiety. Those results belong to that supported treatment, not automatically to this page. The ADIE trial (2021).

Carefully guided work with feared sensations is a separate learning task. The question is what was expected and what actually happened, not how much discomfort someone endured. A longer timer alone does not make such work suitable.

Body-map research with 331 children aged 6–17 found patterns becoming more specific with age. It did not test this training or establish a dose for five-year-olds. Young children explore with a trusted grown-up and their own easy breath. Clear words allow access; each person’s needs guide the practice. Body maps across childhood (2016).

How can we make the next experiment stronger?

Dream Unity research questions: Does keeping love and chest attention together help you sustain a chosen feeling? Does mapping before naming reduce hurried interpretations? Does practising a return of attention help outside a quiet session? These are testable ideas, not proven outcomes.

Choose one question and one result before starting. Repeat both conditions with equal time; vary which comes first. Keep breathing and sound alike. Include unchanged, mixed and uncomfortable results. Check again later, and in a different situation.

A stronger study would compare the full practice with an equally engaging alternative, use suitable body measurements where needed, and check skills beyond the trained task. It would count people who stop as well as people who improve. Your map starts an inquiry. The next observation must remain free to change it.
