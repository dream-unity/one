01 · Breathe

Breathe with ease

Let your breath be small and easy.
Add a kind wish, if you want.

Sit in a way that feels easy. Let your shoulders rest. You can keep your eyes open.

How much time?
Choose an easy pace

If following the circle feels hard, choose your own breath. Slower is not a harder level.

Do not take huge breaths or hold your breath. If you feel dizzy, short of breath or uncomfortable, stop and breathe as usual.

Choose a sound

Sound is optional. Choose what you like and keep it quiet. You can turn all sound off during practice.

What changes between sounds?

The first three use slower pulses, settling at 1, 2 or 3 cycles a second. The next three settle at 8, 10 or 12. The sound slowly changes as it plays. A cycle per second is also called one hertz, or Hz.

These are sound settings, not your brain’s speed. The mix has not been shown to put people into a set brain state. The volume number cannot tell us how loud your headphones are. Read about quiet listening (opens a new tab).

Try both waysA small personal test · 4 minutes, plus a few taps

Try breathing alone and breathing with a kind wish. Make a guess first. Then try each way twice, for one minute each.

Keep the same easy pace and sound. The page picks which way comes first. After each round, tap how easy it felt. “Not clear” and “Skip” both count as honest answers.

You will see your four reports. They cannot prove what caused a change. What you expect, what came before, and changes in breathing may still matter.

Why repeat and change the order?

One way comes first and last. The other comes in the middle. Next time, the page may swap them. Each gets the same practice time.

This helps you question a simple “the last thing worked best” story. It does not remove every effect of order. A kind thought can carry into the next round. You also know which way you are trying.

Decide what you are comparing before you start: how easy the practice feels. That is a report of experience, not a heart, hormone or brain measurement.

Keep asking

Why might this help?

Start with one question. Open another when you want to go further.

What changes when I slow my breath?

Your heart does not leave the same gap between every beat. Breathing helps change those gaps. A slow, easy breath can make the changes follow a more regular wave.

Your body also checks blood pressure and adjusts each beat. At some breathing speeds, these changes line up and make a larger wave. Researchers call this resonance: two rhythms helping each other grow.

Heart rate variability, or HRV, means changes in the gaps between beats. It is not the same as heart rate. This page has no heart sensor. The circle keeps time; it does not show your heartbeat.

Does a smoother heart rhythm mean I feel better?

It can go with feeling better. It is not the same thing. A regular wave does not tell us whether you feel safe, kind, happy or wise.

A review of 31 studies found clearer evidence for changes in heart measures than for an immediate drop in unpleasant feelings. In a 2026 trial, both breathing groups reported fewer symptoms after four weeks, but their resting HRV did not improve.

That is why this practice welcomes “about the same”, “less settled” and “not sure”. A useful report does not need to be a happy report.

What does HeartMath add?

HeartMath brings together easy breathing, attention near the heart, and a feeling such as care or thanks. Here, you can add a kind wish. You can also choose to focus on breathing alone.

A study of about 1.8 million app sessions found that people who reported positive feelings also had higher heart-rhythm scores. These were many sessions from repeated users, not 1.8 million different people.

The study did not randomly give people breath-only or kind-attention practice. It cannot tell us which part caused the difference. Several authors worked with HeartMath. The study is useful evidence of a link, with limits on what that link means.

This is Dream Unity’s adapted practice. It is not a HeartMath device or a measure of “coherence”, the name often used for a regular heart-rhythm pattern.

Why use small, easy breaths?

A slow breath can still be too large. Taking in much more air than you need can make you dizzy or uncomfortable. In a small study of 16 adults, some people breathed too much air even at six breaths a minute.

Let each breath be ordinary in size. Do not fill your lungs as much as you can. There is no breath hold. If the pace feels hard, choose My own breath or stop. You do not need to push through discomfort.

Read the research: Slow breathing and too much air (2019) (opens a new tab)

Do I have to feel love?

No. You can wish yourself, a person, an animal or a plant well. A small kind wish is enough. Nothing special has to happen in your chest.

One study found that pleasant pictures made slow breathing feel easier for some people. It did not prove that privately making a kind feeling adds a special health effect.

If a kind thought feels hard, leave it out. Not feeling warm or happy is not a failure. The “Try both ways” practice lets you compare your own reports without choosing a winner for you.

Think about itAre you noticing what changed, or what you hoped would change?

Read the research: Pleasant pictures and breathing comfort (2012) (opens a new tab)

Can a few minutes make my thinking sharper?

We should not promise that. In a study of 135 young adults with high worry or anxiety scores, slow breathing changed HRV during practice. It did not clearly improve the later thinking task or worry.

Another small study found no overall benefit to attention. Some results may depend on how stressed people were. We need stronger tests before calling this an instant boost to thinking.

You can still practise a useful action: notice when your mind wanders, then choose where to put your attention next.

Think about itWould feeling more able to think and doing better on a thinking task always agree?

Read the research: A breathing and thinking trial (2025) (opens a new tab) · Breathing and attention (2023) (opens a new tab)

How do my heart and brain work together?

Your heart and brain send messages to each other. Nerves inside the heart help control its work. This is real biology. It does not show that the heart stores memories of people you love.

A much-cited study found that rat hearts made oxytocin, a chemical that can send messages in the body. Its often-quoted “19 times” result compared two kinds of tissue in rats. It was not a finding about love stored in human heart tissue.

Studies of adults looking at loved ones found changes in brain activity. They did not test this breathing exercise or measure all the hormone effects sometimes claimed for it.

Can kind practice slow ageing?

Cells hold bundles of instructions called chromosomes. Small caps on their ends are called telomeres. One study of 142 adults looked at changes in these caps. People learning kind-attention meditation lost less telomere length than a waiting group.

Another group learned mindfulness: noticing the present moment. This group fell between the other two groups and did not clearly differ from either. That does not show that kindness works while mindfulness does not. A change in one cell measure also does not prove a longer life.

This result raises a research question. It is not a reason to promise that this exercise heals cells or reverses ageing.

Think about itIf one result is clear and another is unclear, have you shown that the two results differ?

Read the research: Kindness, mindfulness and a cell measure (2019) (opens a new tab)

What do the sound choices do?

They change the sound you hear. Slow pulses and faster pulses are sound choices, not readings of your brain. Choose what you like, keep it quiet, or turn sound off.

A review of 14 brain-recording studies found mixed results from beats made by playing slightly different tones to each ear. Some experiments found brain responses without better mood. A newer study found some attention benefits with different, faster sound settings.

The sound here mixes tones, pulses and soft noise. Those studies do not test this exact mix. No setting here is proven best for sleep, calm or creativity. A brain responding to sound does not by itself mean the sound helped.

Think about itWould you still choose this sound if it had no “brain” label?

Read the research: Mixed results across sound studies (2023) (opens a new tab) · A brain response without a mood change (2020) (opens a new tab) · A newer sound experiment (2025) (opens a new tab)

Is there one best pace or length?

No single pace or length has been shown to be best for everyone. Five seconds in and five seconds out is a common adult practice pace. This page does not find your personal heart-rhythm peak.

In a 2026 trial, 88 adults were assigned to a personal pace, a fixed pace, or a waiting group. Both training groups reported improvements. The study did not find a clear winner between the two paces; its small size also leaves uncertainty.

The short sessions here are choices that make practice easier to start. They are not proven treatment doses. Longer and slower do not automatically mean better.

Children should practise with a trusted grown-up and use their own easy breath. Research with adults does not establish what works for a five-year-old. Easy words do not make an adult breathing pace right for every child.

02 · Notice

Notice your body

Notice a feeling.
Leave room for another explanation.

Choose an easy place to notice, such as a hand or the breath. You can feel the floor or look around instead.

We will guide you through one question at a time. You can answer in your head. Some steps also let you tap an answer.

How much time?

Each round takes four minutes. More rounds add new questions, not stronger sensations.

Breathe as usual. Do not try to make a feared feeling happen. You can pause, look around or finish at any time.

Keep asking

A feeling is a clue.

What did you notice? What did you guess? What else could fit?

What am I learning to notice?

Your brain keeps track of changes inside your body. The name for sensing these inner changes is interoception. Breath, hunger and the heartbeat are examples.

In this practice you also use touch, the floor, sights and sounds. These are helpful ways to place attention. They are not all signals from inside your organs.

The aim is to notice a little, leave room for doubt, and choose what to do next. You do not need to search for a strong or unusual feeling.

Does one body feeling have one meaning?

No. A fast heart can go with running, worry or excitement. A tight feeling does not, by itself, tell you its cause. Look at what else is happening.

In studies with 701 people, researchers found shared patterns in drawings of where emotions were felt. These were reports from groups. They were not organ measurements or a private code that can tell you exactly what another person feels.

You can try a name such as “worried” and still leave room to change your mind. You can also say, “I do not know yet.”

Think about itIf two different causes can make the same feeling, what second clue could help you tell them apart?

Read the research: People’s drawings of body feelings (2014) (opens a new tab)

What is the difference between strong, clear and sure?

Strong means how big a sensation feels. Clear means how well you can describe it. Sure means how much you trust your answer. These can differ.

A strong feeling can be hard to describe. A faint feeling can seem clear. You can feel very sure and still be wrong about the cause.

To measure accuracy, researchers need a suitable check outside the person’s guess. This page has no such body measurement. Your taps describe your experience; they do not earn a body-reading score.

Why look away and then come back?

Attention can stay on one thing or move to another. Here, moving it is part of the practice. Look at a colour, hear a sound, then return only if you want.

A study of everyday feelings linked inward attention with several ways of handling emotions, including both looking at a problem in a new way and going over worries. It did not show that more body checking is always better.

When you return, the feeling may seem the same, different or unclear. The body may have changed. Your attention or memory may have changed too. Matching your first report does not prove accuracy.

Think about itCan looking away be the more skilful choice right now?

Read the research: Attention and everyday emotion habits (2022) (opens a new tab)

Does body practice make people more accurate?

The findings are mixed. One small heartbeat-training study found an early change, but no clear lasting advantage after three weeks.

In a body-scan study, the practice did not clearly beat listening to an audiobook on the main change in accuracy. It did better against a group that did no training. What you compare an exercise with matters.

Older studies often used heartbeat counting. That task can reward knowing or guessing a usual heart rate. We do not use it here as an accuracy score.

Think about itDid practice help, or did time, attention and having something to do help?

Can this kind of training help with anxiety?

Some carefully supported training has helped particular groups. A trial with 121 autistic adults found a modest anxiety benefit from training with real heartbeat feedback.

Only half the participants supplied the three-month follow-up. One person reported increased anxiety about the heart. The training used equipment and activities that are not part of this page.

This supports further research. It does not show that our steps treat anxiety. We keep the target gentle, include looking outward, and let you finish without having to face or increase a feared sensation.

Does one body skill carry over to every other one?

Not necessarily. A person can do well on one body task and less well on another. In a 2026 study of 241 people, most measures of sensing heart and breathing signals did not go together.

How sure people felt was shared more than several measures of performance. Confidence may travel further than the evidence that supports it.

The study compared people at one time. It did not prove that learning can never carry over. Transfer is a question to test, not a prize this page can promise.

Think about itIf you get better at noticing breath, how would you test whether that helps you notice hunger?

Read the research: Heart and breathing skills compared (2026) (opens a new tab) · Six kinds of body sensing (2018) (opens a new tab)

Would perfect body sensing make me feel well?

It is not that simple. A 2026 study tested heart sensing in 456 people and breathing sensing in 245. Most measured performance was not linked to mental-health symptoms.

People’s beliefs about their body awareness were more closely linked with symptoms. The wording of those reports may explain some of that link.

This does not rule out useful body practice. It does challenge the idea that higher sensing scores must mean better mental health. How you interpret a feeling and respond to it may matter too.

Think about itCould someone notice a feeling accurately, yet give it an unhelpful meaning?

Read the research: Body-task performance and mental health (2026) (opens a new tab)

Can a word change the feeling I am trying to name?

A word can help you make sense of a feeling. It can also lead your attention. Calling a sensation “danger” and calling it “getting ready” may invite different next steps.

A brain-imaging experiment found that naming emotions changed responses to negative pictures. It did not prove that a label reveals the true cause of a body feeling.

Try a name lightly. Then ask what else fits and what new clue would change your mind. You can keep the sensation real while keeping the explanation open.

Think about itAm I using this word because it fits, or does the feeling seem to fit because I used this word?

Read the research: Naming feelings in a picture task (2007) (opens a new tab)

How could these exercises be tested better?

Researchers could compare the full practice with an equally clear exercise of the same length. They would decide what “help” means before seeing results.

They could check whether benefits last, whether people can move attention by choice, and whether a skill helps in a new task. They would record discomfort and people who stop, as well as people who improve.

Our new step sequence and personal comparison are learning designs, not proven treatments. Researchers have not yet tested this exact set of steps against another exercise. The open questions are part of the work.
